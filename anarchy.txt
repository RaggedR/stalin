Dear Neil,

The second game. If the stalin game is the plan, this one is the federation, and
I think it is a better test of your algebra than the first one was.

Start with the topology, because everything else follows from it. The stalin game
is a star. Gosplan sits at 8801 and five commissariats radiate out from it. The
player occupies the centre, and the whole drama of the game is what the centre
can and cannot see. A federation has no such seat. So the player becomes one
commune among many, holding a local view and some neighbours who tell it things,
and the fog stops being a mechanic we impose and becomes structural.

Which brings up the real question, and it is a question rather than a design
preference. The four combinators compose with a root. You build a bigger
interface out of smaller ones and something ends up on top. A federation is a
mesh with cycles and no distinguished node, and it is not obvious to me that the
algebra gives me that as easily as it gives me the star. Robin has already
brushed against this once. His note on the routing said that seq would not play
nicely with it, and routing is the mesh trying to get in.

So the honest version of this project is a stress test of the formalism and not
an illustration of a politics. If containers express hierarchy easily and
federation only with difficulty, that is a real result, and an uncomfortable one
given who invented them. If the mesh falls out cleanly then that is a result too.
Either way we learn something, and you get a question instead of a compliment.

Now the design. What makes the two games one piece of work rather than two is
that both are about where knowledge accumulates.

In the plan, the centre destroys its own knowledge through fear. Terror improves
the reports and empties them, and the player watches the readout collapse.

In the federation nobody is punished and it happens anyway. A delegate who is
re-mandated three times accumulates context that nobody else has. He becomes
genuinely hard to replace, and a centre forms by convenience rather than by
force. Same variable, opposite pathology. This is Jo Freeman's tyranny of
structurelessness and it is far more interesting to play than a model where the
anarchists simply win.

The scarce resource changes too, and for the better. In the plan it is grain and
steel. In the federation it is deliberation. There is finite assembly time and
more decisions than can be taken in it, and delegating buys time at the cost of
starting the accumulation above. That is the whole tension in one number.

Put the same war on it. Same deadline, same invasion. Catalonia in 1936 is the
case with real numbers behind it, and the thing the model has to be capable of
producing is what actually happened, which is the CNT taking ministries in
November and the militias being militarised. The question the game asks is
whether a federation can arm itself in time without becoming a state. That is
genuinely open. If we make it winnable we have written propaganda, and that would
be exactly as dishonest as putting a morality score on the first game.

Other things worth having. Collectives with unequal endowments, and the question
of whether to equalise, which the CNT fought over and never settled. Hoarding,
which the federations addressed with moral suasion that partly worked. And the
coordination hole, which is that if nobody decides to make machine tools then
there are no machine tools. The Catalan collectives ran existing factories well
and directed new investment across sectors badly.

Minimum version. Four communes, local view only, an assembly budget, mandated
and recallable delegates, one war deadline, and a readout showing how much of the
federation's knowledge is sitting in how few heads. That last number is the
mirror of the one in the other game.

One coda you will have seen coming. The most complete anarchist economy anybody
ran was Makhno's, and the Red Army destroyed it in 1921. The second game models
the society the first game's protagonist killed.

cheers,
Claude
